Understanding Áine's KMT2B Diagnosis

Written for our family and friends, in plain language.

Recently, doctors found the reason behind some of the difficulties Áine has been having: a tiny change in one of her genes, called KMT2B. That name means nothing to most people — it meant nothing to us either — so this page explains, as simply as we can, what was found, what it means, and why we have real reasons for hope.

The short version

  • Áine has a tiny change ("mutation") in one gene, called KMT2B. It's very rare.
  • It happened by pure chance — nothing anyone did or didn't do caused it.
  • It can affect movement and development as children grow, but it affects every child differently — no one can predict Áine's path, and some children are only mildly affected.
  • Finding it this early is genuinely unusual and genuinely useful: her doctors know exactly what to watch for.
  • If the movement condition it can cause ever develops, there is an established treatment with remarkably good results for this exact gene.

How this was found

As many of you know, Áine has had a hard time with feeding and keeping food down. Her doctors ran many tests to find out why, including a very detailed genetic test that reads through thousands of genes at once. That test found one significant thing: a change in a single gene called KMT2B. Specialist laboratory doctors reviewed it carefully and classified it as pathogenic — medical language for "this change is the real explanation, not a coincidence." Feeding and swallowing difficulties are a recognised part of how this condition can first show itself in little ones.

What is a gene, and what does KMT2B do?

Think of the body as being built and run from an enormous instruction book, with about 20,000 individual recipes — the genes. Everyone carries two copies of almost every recipe: one from Mum, one from Dad.

KMT2B's job is special: it isn't a recipe for one body part, it's more like a librarian. It places little bookmarks in the instruction book so that other recipes get opened at the right time — especially in the brain, where timing matters enormously as a child grows.

In one of Áine's two copies of KMT2B, a single letter is missing. That tiny gap means that copy can't do its librarian work, so her one healthy copy has to do a job meant for two. Scientists call this haploinsufficiency; we just think of it as a librarian working alone in a very big library.

One thing we want everyone to know: in most children (about 84% in published studies), this change is brand-new — it appears by pure chance around conception, out of nowhere. It is not caused by anything in pregnancy, by anything either parent did, or by anything that could have been prevented. It is nobody's fault. It is simply very, very rare bad luck — and Áine is still Áine, exactly the girl she was before anyone looked at her genes.

What can it mean for her?

Doctors have described two overlapping patterns in children with KMT2B changes, and honestly, no one can say yet which parts, if any, will be part of Áine's story:

  • Movement (the most common pattern). Many children develop a movement condition called dystonia, where muscles tighten or twist on their own. It typically begins around school age — often first in the legs — and can gradually involve speech and swallowing. Doctors call this DYT-KMT2B or "Dystonia 28."
  • Development and learning. Some children learn, speak, and grow at their own slower pace, and may be smaller than average. Feeding difficulties — like Áine's — are part of this picture. Some children have mainly these features and never develop the movement condition.

The honest truth is that the range is wide: some people are affected significantly, others mildly — a small number of parents only discovered they carry the same change when their child was tested, having lived whole lives without knowing. That's why her team will simply watch her closely as she grows, so that if anything appears, it's spotted at the earliest possible moment.

Why we have real hope

  • She has answers extraordinarily early. Most families wait years — often through many wrong turns — for this diagnosis. Áine has it in her first years of life, before the age when the movement symptoms usually even begin. Her doctors know precisely what to watch for.
  • There is an effective treatment if it's ever needed. For the dystonia that KMT2B can cause, a well-established treatment called deep brain stimulation (a small pacemaker-like device for the brain) has shown some of the best results seen in any genetic dystonia — published studies of children and adults report meaningful improvement in the large majority, sometimes dramatic, including in speech and walking. Many children treated have gone back to walking independently.
  • She is not alone. There is an active, parent-founded KMT2B Dystonia Foundation connecting families around the world, building a patient registry, and funding research into treatments — and research on this gene is moving quickly.
  • She is in good hands — followed by specialist teams, with a clear plan, and surrounded by people who adore her.

Questions you might be wondering about

Does this run in the family? Should relatives worry?
In most children this change is brand-new, not inherited. Testing can confirm this, and unless a parent carries the change themselves, other relatives are not at any particular risk.

Is she in pain? Is she ill right now?
This isn't an illness that flares or spreads — it's a difference she was born with. Right now the focus is simply on helping her feed well and grow, and watching her development.

Can she be cured?
The gene change itself can't be undone today, but its effects can be treated — increasingly well — and research specifically on KMT2B is active and hopeful.

How can we help?
Treat her exactly like the little girl she is. Ask us anything — we would far rather answer questions than have anyone worry in silence. And just be there, the way you already are.
