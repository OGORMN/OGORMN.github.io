Understanding Áine's KMT2B Diagnosis

A plain-English explainer for family and friends.

Late last year, doctors found the reason Áine has been slower than expected to put on weight: a tiny change in one of her genes, called KMT2B. The name meant nothing to us either. This page explains what was found, what it means, and — just as importantly — what it doesn't.

The short version

  • Áine has a small change ("mutation") in one gene, called KMT2B. It's very rare.
  • It's confirmed de novo — brand-new in her. We were both tested, and neither of us carries it. Pure chance, nobody's doing.
  • At 13 months she's walking, bright, happy and into everything. She's just small, and getting enough calories into her takes work.
  • The gene change can affect movement and development as children grow, but the range is very wide and nobody can predict her path.
  • Finding it this early is unusual, and useful — her doctors know exactly what to watch for.
  • If she ever develops the movement condition associated with KMT2B, there are established treatments that can help significantly.

How Áine is doing

First, the important bit: Áine is 13 months old, walking since just before her first birthday, sharp as a tack and busy from morning to night. She isn't sick. The one thing that's been hard work is weight.

She was born at 2.7 kg, just a week early. Over her first months her weight drifted down the growth charts and eventually below the bottom line, which is when doctors apply the label "failure to thrive." It's an ugly phrase for what is really a note about growth charts — but it's the label that set the investigations going.

She was breastfed, and she fed well — it just didn't seem to be enough to grow on. On top of that she wouldn't take a bottle, so for most of her first year a feeding tube topped her up, with a high-energy formula (Infantrini) doing the heavy lifting. She started solids at about six months and took to them well — able and willing from the start. Since around her first birthday she's come on further still: she drinks from a straw, manages a sippy cup, and is even doing better with the bottle now. The underlying puzzle is unchanged — she seems to need more calories than you'd expect to keep growing.

The tube came out last week so she can start crèche. She had got up to 7.1 kg; a vaccine, teething and a dose of diarrhoea all in the same stretch knocked her back to 6.7 kg, so the current project is building that back up, with an energy supplement (Calogen) alongside her meals.

How this was found

Because her weight kept sliding despite everyone's best efforts, her doctors ran a series of tests, ending with a very detailed genetic test that reads through thousands of genes at once. It found one significant thing: a change in a single gene called KMT2B. Specialist laboratory doctors reviewed it and classified it as pathogenic — medical shorthand for "this is the real explanation, not a coincidence." Feeding difficulties and slow growth are a recognised way this condition first shows itself in small children.

What is a gene, and what does KMT2B do?

Think of the body as being built and run from an enormous instruction book, with about 20,000 individual recipes — the genes. Everyone carries two copies of almost every recipe, one from each parent.

KMT2B's job is a bit different: it isn't a recipe for one body part, it's more like a librarian. It places bookmarks in the instruction book so that other recipes get opened at the right time — especially in the brain, where timing matters a great deal as a child grows.

In one of Áine's two copies of KMT2B, a single letter is missing. That tiny gap means that copy can't do its librarian work, so her one healthy copy is doing a job meant for two. Scientists call this haploinsufficiency; a librarian working alone in a very big library covers it.

Worth knowing: Áine's change is confirmed de novo — brand-new in her. We were both tested, and neither of us carries it. It happened by chance around conception; it wasn't caused by anything in pregnancy or anything anyone did or didn't do. And she's the same girl she was before anyone read her genes.

What can it mean for her?

Doctors have described two overlapping patterns in children with KMT2B changes. Nobody can say yet which parts, if any, will be part of Áine's story:

  • Movement (the most common pattern). Many children develop a movement condition called dystonia, where muscles tighten or twist on their own. It typically begins around school age — often first in the legs — and can gradually involve speech and swallowing. Doctors call this DYT-KMT2B or "Dystonia 28."
  • Development and learning. Some children learn, speak and grow at their own slower pace, and may be smaller than average — being small from birth, and feeding difficulties like Áine's, are part of this recognised picture. Some children have mainly these features and never develop the movement condition.

The honest truth is that the range is wide: some people are affected significantly, others mildly — a small number of adults have only discovered they carry the same change when their child was tested, having lived whole lives without knowing. So far, Áine is doing what one-year-olds do. The gene tells her doctors what to watch for; it doesn't tell anyone what will happen. That's why her team will simply keep a close eye on her as she grows.

Medical details & research

The variant. NM_014727.3(KMT2B): c.4951del, p.(Val1651Trpfs*45), heterozygous, in exon 23 of KMT2B (chromosome 19q13.12; genomic position chr19:36,220,899 on GRCh37). Confirmed de novo: both parents tested negative on blood samples.

What it does. A single guanine is deleted in exon 23, shifting the reading frame at valine 1651. The shifted frame runs into a premature stop signal 45 codons later — far short of the full ~2,700-amino-acid protein. Transcripts with a premature stop this early are generally destroyed by the cell's quality control (nonsense-mediated decay), so this copy of the gene most likely produces no protein at all. The result is haploinsufficiency — one working copy instead of two — which is the established disease mechanism in KMT2B-related disorders, and protein-truncating variants like this one are the most commonly reported type.

Classification. Pathogenic under ACMG/ACGS criteria: PVS1 (a null variant in a gene where loss of function is the known mechanism) plus PM2 — the variant is absent from gnomAD, the reference database of more than 800,000 sequenced individuals worldwide.

The differential, for completeness. The chapter lists dozens of other genetic movement and neurodevelopmental disorders that can resemble KMT2B-related disease (Wilson disease, the dopamine-synthesis disorders, DYT1/TOR1A, Silver-Russell and Kleefstra syndromes among them). In Áine's case these were effectively covered by the diagnostic route itself: exome sequencing surveys those genes in the same test, her earlier chromosomal microarray was normal, and the KMT2B variant was the single significant finding.

Has anyone else had this exact variant? Not that we can find. It does not appear in the published literature, and the laboratory's scoring is consistent with it being previously unreported. In genetics jargon it appears to be private — unique to Áine. That is the norm for this gene rather than the exception: reported KMT2B truncating variants are scattered along its length, and most families carry their own. What is shared across families is the mechanism (one non-working copy), so the published natural history and treatment outcomes apply to her case regardless of the exact spelling.

How rare is rare? As of the May 2025 update of GeneReviews, 258 individuals from 229 families had been described worldwide with the dystonia presentation (DYT-KMT2B), and 27 individuals from 25 families with the non-dystonic neurodevelopmental presentation — roughly 285 people in the world's medical literature, across Europe, the Americas, Asia and Australia. The true number is certainly higher (mild and undiagnosed cases exist, and known families now number in the hundreds), but it remains a condition most doctors will never see. Paradoxically, among the genetic causes of childhood-onset generalised dystonia, KMT2B is one of the most frequently found.

What her variant class suggests. In published cohorts, protein-truncating variants like Áine's are on average associated with earlier onset of the movement disorder than missense variants (one study: mean 4.4 vs 8.7 years); reports differ on whether they mean more severe dystonia. Two points worth underlining: the class of variant does not appear to influence the rate of progression or the overall response to deep brain stimulation; and when developmental delay occurs it usually precedes any dystonia and is thought to be non-progressive, with most individuals with intellectual disability going on to develop variable functional independence in adolescence and adulthood. For context, dystonia onset is typically in the first decade (median age 6), but onset in the teens and well into adulthood — as late as the sixth decade — is described.

Penetrance. Not everyone who carries a pathogenic KMT2B variant develops symptoms: reduced penetrance is documented, and in one series 2 of 32 reported variants had been inherited from a seemingly unaffected parent.

A note for anyone googling the gene. KMT2B also shows up in the cancer-genomics literature. Those reports concern mutations acquired by tumour cells (somatic changes) — a different phenomenon entirely. The chapter is explicit that such tumours are not heritable and that a germline variant like Áine's is not known to carry any predisposition to them.

Treatment facts, in numbers. Anticholinergic (antimuscarinic) medicines significantly improve motor symptoms in about half of treated individuals and are considered the first-line drug option (started low, titrated slowly). Bilateral globus pallidus interna deep brain stimulation has been performed in about 80 individuals to date: in the 45 with published scores, it reduced dystonia motor scores by a mean of ~49% (range 2–98%) within 2–16 months and disability scores by ~38%, with benefit sustained at five-year follow-up. It works best for trunk, neck and arm involvement, less well for lower-limb and voice involvement; freezing of gait is increasingly recognised as a late complication, reported more often in individuals with truncating variants. Early physiotherapy and a tailored exercise programme are recommended in their own right — they maintain function and help prevent orthopaedic complications. Brain MRI, for what it's worth, is reported normal in roughly 40% of individuals, and shows only subtle changes in most of the rest.

What routine follow-up covers. The chapter's surveillance table recommends: neurology review two to four times a year (tone and movement); growth, weight and nutrition checks at every visit — the drill Áine's family already knows well; feeding-safety and developmental review at every visit; speech and language review; musculoskeletal checks (contractures, scoliosis); eye checks annually (visual acuity, refractive error, squint); behavioural screening as she grows; and family support and care coordination throughout. The standard supportive tools are feeding therapy from occupational or speech therapists, dietician input on calorie needs, and — should swallowing safety ever be in question — a videofluoroscopy swallow study.

Growth and hormones. Being born small (intrauterine growth restriction) and short stature are recognised features of the neurodevelopmental presentation, and occasional endocrine issues (under-active thyroid, early puberty) are reported — so growth and hormone checks are part of sensible follow-up. Áine's smallness sits squarely within this known picture rather than being a mystery on top of it.

Therapeutic outlook for haploinsufficiency. Because the mechanism is a dosage deficit with an intact wild-type allele, KMT2B is in the category of disorders considered well suited to allele-upregulation strategies. Antisense oligonucleotide approaches (including TANGO-type augmentation of productive splicing, in clinical trials for SCN1A/Dravet haploinsufficiency) have been systematically mapped onto the KMT2-associated disorders (Zardetto et al, 2024), and a 2025 analysis estimates roughly a third of neurodevelopmental disorders — haploinsufficiency conditions foremost — are tractable to ASO-based treatment. Partial restoration of expression may suffice; full wild-type dosage is not necessarily required. No KMT2B-directed therapy is in the clinic to date.

Outlook data. Life expectancy has not been formally established — largely because the condition was only genetically defined in 2016 — but individuals in their seventh decade of life are described in the literature.

Inheritance notes. Because the variant arose de novo and both parents tested negative, the chance of it recurring in a future pregnancy is very low — around 1% is usually quoted, to allow for the rare situation where a variant is present in a small fraction of a parent's egg or sperm cells (germline mosaicism). Any children Áine herself may one day have would each carry a 50% chance of inheriting the variant; prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing are available options at that stage.

Reasons to be positive

  • She has answers unusually early. Most families wait years for this diagnosis, often through plenty of wrong turns. Áine has hers in her first year — before the age when the movement symptoms usually even begin — so nothing will catch her doctors by surprise.
  • There are effective treatments if they're ever needed. For the dystonia that KMT2B can cause, medicines meaningfully improve symptoms in about half of those treated, and a well-established procedure called deep brain stimulation (a small pacemaker-like device) has now been performed in about 80 people with this condition, with substantial and lasting improvement reported for most. The detailed numbers — including what it helps most and least — are in the medical section above.
  • She's not the only one. A parent-founded KMT2B Dystonia Foundation connects families internationally, runs a patient registry, and funds research — and research on this gene is moving quickly.
  • There's a plan. She's followed by specialist teams, with regular check-ups and a clear idea of what to look out for.

Questions you might have

Does this run in the family? Should relatives worry?
No. The change arose new in Áine — both of us tested clear — so there is no reason for grandparents, aunts, uncles or cousins to worry about carrying the same inherited change.

Is she ill right now?
No. She's a happy, active one-year-old. The current work is practical: calories in, weight up, regular check-ups.

Can she be cured?
Not today. The gene change itself can't be undone, and no treatment directed at the gene exists yet — research in that direction is active and genuinely encouraging, but it is research, not something around the corner. What can be treated, increasingly well, are the effects if they ever arise, and that is what her care is built on. For anyone curious, the fuller research picture is in the medical section above.

How can we help?
Treat her like any other one-year-old, because that's what she is. And if you're curious about any of this, just ask — we'd rather questions than worried silence.
